Smart Pickup Station Package Audit System

Used a vision-language model to automate pickup auditing, then reduced inference cost by about 73% through model replacement and prompt optimization after customer feedback.
▾
Problem

An unmanned pickup station needed to verify whether a customer actually went to the scanner before leaving. The original process relied on human agents watching video, which did not scale, while real-world movement and occlusion made pure rule-based logic insufficient.

Solution

I combined a Gemini VLM with a two-stage geofence state machine: Stage 1 detects pickup behavior near the shelf, Stage 2 checks scanning near the scanner. Object detection provides person coordinates, custom collision logic handles zone entry, and the VLM performs semantic confirmation. After the customer flagged API cost, I switched to Qwen-VL, reworked prompts, and re-tested 45 scenarios to choose the best cost/performance balance.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Cost optimization: Replaced the VLM with a lightweight 8B model and cropped the scanner area before Stage 2, helping the smaller model focus on small objects while improving both accuracy and cost.
  • Robustness: Person detection can flicker in surveillance footage, so I added tolerance for missed frames without resetting timers plus an LLM cooldown to prevent repeated triggers.
  • Fallback design: Implemented voiceprint matching as a fallback path to assist support staff when the VLM or network is unavailable.
Outcome

Inference cost was reduced to about TWD 0.0035 per transaction, roughly 73% lower than the original Gemini approach. I validated the solution on 45 effective test scenarios and produced a test report.

Contract Risk Analysis Tool

Benchmarked model/flow options, then used a fast full-document model plus local clause-number correction to bring large-contract analysis down to a usable 20–60 seconds.
▾
Problem

Legal reviewers had to read contracts of roughly 50,000 Chinese characters line by line, which was slow and error-prone. Sending the entire contract to the AI service could hit a ~100-second gateway timeout, and the model often cited the wrong clause number.

Solution

I systematically evaluated 10 LLMs under two strategies—single-pass full document and chunk-then-aggregate—tracking latency, clause-hit accuracy, and risk-level distribution. The selected approach was a fast full-document call with timeout retry plus local clause-number verification, presented through a one-page risk dashboard.

Use Case

The goal of this demo is not to replace legal reviewers, but to perform a first-pass risk scan, highlight potentially important clauses and their original positions, and help legal staff focus faster while retaining final judgment.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Data-driven model selection: Fast full-document runs took 20–60 seconds, outperforming slower chunked flows at 3.5–23 minutes; the aggregation step also timed out and biased risk levels upward.
  • Clause correction: A weighted character n-gram method matches the model-cited clause back to the original text and only overwrites it when another clause has clearly stronger evidence. It adds zero API cost and corrected 10/10 tested cases.
  • Built a local CORS proxy so the browser front end could access the backend AI service.
Outcome

Converted contracts of ~50,000 characters into a table summary that points directly to risky clauses, reducing first-pass reading time. Clause verification runs locally with no additional API cost.

OCR Document Image Quality Checker

Starting from false positives and false negatives on real images, I repeatedly performed failure analysis and regression testing, eventually replacing an unstable hard-crop design with soft masks and per-metric ROIs.
▾
Problem

OCR preprocessing initially looked like a simple set of checks for resolution, blur, exposure, skew, and text size. In practice, phone photos, scans, white/black desks, barcode/QR regions, different resolutions, and cluttered backgrounds caused many false positives and false negatives.

Representative Debug

The first skew detector filtered Hough lines to ±30°, creating blind spots around 45° and 60°. After switching to orthogonal-axis mapping, real phone images still slipped through. The root cause turned out to be an overly high Hough threshold on high-resolution images, leaving no usable lines. I then introduced stepped thresholds and robust aggregation.

Architecture Evolution

The larger issue was not any single threshold, but that all quality metrics depended on the same hard-crop result. Small input differences could cause the crop ratio to jump, which in turn made blur, skew, and exposure measurements drift. I replaced that architecture with a soft mask so blur, skew, and text-size checks each use their own ROI filtering.

Current Coverage
  • Resolution、Blur、Brightness、Glare、Underexposure、Skew、Text size、Content size。
  • Real-image and synthetic testing, threshold analysis, debug metrics, and regression testing.
  • Known limitations and unresolved edge cases are documented instead of presenting the system as fully solved.

Telegram Product Bug Reporting Bot

Turned the fragmented habit of “message an engineer” into a low-friction event-driven workflow for reporting, archiving, assignment, status sync, and closure notifications—all within the team’s existing Telegram habits.
▾
Problem

Bug reports were scattered across messages, email, and spreadsheets, while status tracking and closure notifications back to the original reporter often broke down.

Solution

Used an FSM to guide report submission step by step, route records into product-specific Google Sheets, upload images, notify engineering groups with the responsible owner, and use Apps Script to detect status changes and automatically notify the original reporter, completing the loop.

Product Design Trade-off

The company already used Telegram heavily, so I did not build a separate reporting app. The goal was to change the behavior from “message an engineer” to “message the bot,” rather than force everyone to learn a new ticket system. The real challenge was workflow design, not model complexity.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Config-driven design: Products, modules, owners, and allowlists live in configuration sheets. New products can be added without code changes, with /reload hot reload without restart.
  • Permission trade-off: Cloud-drive permission constraints led to using an image host; notification scripts were moved from simple triggers to installable triggers because external requests were not allowed.
  • Separation of concerns (SoC): The code is separated into configuration, data model, service, and conversation-flow layers for clearer maintenance.
Outcome

Delivered a handoff-ready product with an architecture diagram, maintenance guide, from-scratch setup instructions, v1→v2 evolution notes, and a list of known limitations.

Future

Future idea: let users describe issues in natural language, then have an LLM extract product, issue description, reproduction steps, and usage context into the existing workflow. Not yet implemented.

Assembly-Line Screw Error-Proofing System

Independently delivered an end-to-end assembly-line error-proofing PoC in 8 working days and distilled the approach into a reusable FSM example and SOP.
▾
Problem

The assembly process lacked automatic checks, so missing or misplaced screws could cause a tray to be scrapped. Factory cameras also shift physically and hands occlude the tray, making fixed-coordinate logic unreliable.

Solution

Trained one YOLO model for screws and another for hands, then connected them with a three-stage FSM (wait for pickup → enter tray → leave and settle) to compare the source bin with the newly occupied tray position and raise an alert on mismatch.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Used phase correlation to estimate physical tray displacement, transform current detections back into the reference coordinate system, and solve camera-shift issues.
  • The first hand model only labeled hands over the tray, so pickup actions near source bins were missed. I added “no-hand / pickup” cases and retrained the model.
  • The same FSM approach was later reused on another PCB production line with dual geofences, demonstrating portability.
Outcome

Delivered the full pipeline independently: extracted frames from real customer assembly footage, cropped trays, labeled and trained a screw-presence YOLO, labeled and trained a hand detector, then integrated both with the FSM. Around 650 training samples were used, with solid model performance.

CAD Drawing Version Difference Detection

Converted million-scale CAD drawings into structured data and used geometric fingerprints plus set operations to identify version differences in 4 minutes.
▾
Problem

Engineering drawing revisions require version-by-version comparison. AutoCAD has built-in comparison, but it becomes extremely slow on very large drawings, while the files are too large for direct LLM input or real-time rendered comparison.

Solution

Built a DWG→DXF→CSV conversion pipeline. Using ezdxf, I exploded nested blocks, discretized curves, split polylines, and flattened objects into relational tables. Each object received a geometric fingerprint, and set operations identified added, removed, and unchanged objects for visualization.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Rejected text-summary-to-LLM and rendered-image comparison: the first loses information, while the second is too slow at million-object scale. Switched to fingerprint comparison as the scalable approach.
  • Rounded fingerprint coordinates to four decimal places to eliminate false differences caused by floating-point noise.
  • Debugged object-type-specific iteration behavior in the parsing library, which had once caused 1.14M objects to collapse to only 6 detected items.
Outcome

Parsed 15.38M geometric objects in 4 minutes with a 99.7% capture rate and no out-of-memory failure, greatly reducing manual comparison time (estimated from the manual workflow).

Document AI — Output Design and Capability Boundaries for Small OCR Models

Tested Markdown, HTML, JSON, and plain-text output strategies across documents, business cards, tables, invoices, and complex layouts to find a practical balance between structure fidelity, output length, and downstream parsing reliability.
▾
Problem

When enterprises digitize paper documents, invoices, business cards, and tables often have irregular layouts that are difficult to parse automatically, forcing manual data entry.

Solution

For documents, business cards, tables, and invoices, I designed minimal prompts and output formats aligned with the model’s strengths: Markdown + HTML tables + LaTeX for documents, flat JSON for business cards, HTML for tables, and either JSON or plain text for invoices. I also wrote backend handoff specifications.

Key Technical Decisions
  • Used positive rules and output schemas instead of negative prohibitions, enforcing structural consistency so the backend could parse results directly.
  • Complex financial tables could overwhelm the small model, so I proposed backend-side OpenCV region cropping before inference.
  • Added guard-string interception so fixed model error messages would not crash downstream parsing.
Outcome

Delivered stable formats and specifications that the backend could parse directly, allowing data to enter the database without manual intervention. For expense invoices, key fields could be extracted automatically after upload; for business cards, sales teams could quickly build contact records. The prompts converged after more than 50 iterations.

How the Projects Connect

Document AI exposed another gap: enterprise inputs are not always clean text. They may be scanned PDFs, images, complex tables, or irregular layouts, making OCR quality checks, document parsing, and output schemas important upstream layers.

The Telegram Bug Bot taught me a different side of workflow design: value comes not only from model output, but from whether events, states, notifications, ownership, and human feedback form a complete operational loop.
